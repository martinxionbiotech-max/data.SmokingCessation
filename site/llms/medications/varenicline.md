# Varenicline

## What it is

A prescription medication (marketed as Chantix/Champix) developed specifically for smoking cessation. It is a partial agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

## How it works

It binds to the same brain receptors as nicotine. It partially stimulates them — reducing withdrawal and craving — while blocking nicotine from fully activating them, which reduces the reward of smoking.

## What the evidence shows

- Cochrane's review of nicotine receptor partial agonists found varenicline increases quit rates substantially versus placebo (roughly 2–3x) — see [R-002](../research/records/r-002.md).
- The EAGLES trial (8,144 participants, with and without psychiatric disorders) found **no significant increase in moderate-to-severe neuropsychiatric adverse events** versus placebo or patch, and varenicline was the most effective of the compared treatments — see [R-015](../research/records/r-015.md).
- The 2016 network meta-analysis overview placed varenicline among the most effective single medications — see [R-008](../research/records/r-008.md).
- WHO (2024), USPSTF (2021) and NICE (NG209) all recommend varenicline.

## Common considerations

- Nausea is the most commonly reported side effect, usually mild and early in treatment.
- Abnormal dreams and insomnia are also commonly reported.
- Following the EAGLES results, regulators removed the black-box neuropsychiatric warning. People with a history of psychiatric illness were included in the trial; individual assessment still matters.
- Availability varies by country and by supply history; generic forms exist in several markets.

## Questions to ask a healthcare professional

- Is varenicline suitable given my medical history and current medications?
- What should I expect in the first weeks, and what side effects should I report?
- How does it compare with NRT or cytisine for me specifically?
