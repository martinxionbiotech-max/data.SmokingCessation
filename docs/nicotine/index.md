# Nicotine: What It Is and How Dependence Works

Understanding nicotine is the foundation of understanding quitting. This page covers the pharmacology — what nicotine does in the brain, why dependence develops, and why withdrawal happens.

## What nicotine is

Nicotine is the primary addictive substance in tobacco. It is a stimulant alkaloid that reaches the brain within seconds when inhaled in cigarette smoke — faster than intravenous administration.

## How dependence develops

- Nicotine binds to **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors**, releasing dopamine in reward pathways. The fast spike and rapid clearance of cigarette-delivered nicotine makes the reinforcement loop unusually strong.
- With repeated use, the brain **adapts**: receptor numbers change, and normal function comes to depend on nicotine. This is neuroadaptation — the biological core of dependence.
- Behavioural conditioning then layers on top: lighting up becomes paired with coffee, driving, stress, alcohol, work breaks. The habit network is what keeps triggering urges long after the biology settles.

## Why withdrawal happens

When nicotine is removed, the adapted brain is temporarily out of balance. The mismatch between a brain "expecting" nicotine and not receiving it produces the withdrawal syndrome: irritability, craving, low mood, difficulty concentrating, sleep disturbance, increased appetite.

- Most physical withdrawal symptoms **peak in the first week and substantially resolve within 2–4 weeks** — see the Hughes withdrawal time-course record [R-007](../research/records/r-007.md).
- **Craving** behaves differently: it can persist for months, in waves, especially when conditioned triggers are encountered.

## Key distinction: dependence vs habit

| | Dependence | Conditioned habit |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Neuroadaptation requiring nicotine | Learned associations triggering urge |
| Timescale | Resolves over weeks | Weakens over months–years, by repetition |
| Treated by | NRT, varenicline, bupropion, cytisine | Behavioural change, trigger management |

Both matter, and they are treated differently — which is why combined medication + behavioural support outperforms either alone.
